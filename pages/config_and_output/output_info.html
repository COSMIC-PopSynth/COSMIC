Understanding COSMIC outputs¶

Since COSMIC uses BSE as it’s core binary evolution algorithm, the output of COSMIC follows most of the same conventions as BSE. The kstar values and evolution stages are nearly identical to their BSE counterparts.

You can learn more about how to analyze the output of COSMIC in the Analysing your simulations section.

kick_info - Table of natal kick information¶

kick_info is a (2,19) array that tracks information about supernova kicks. This allows us to track the total change to the systemic velocity and the total change in the orbital plane tilt after both supernovae, as well as reproduce systems. Each row contains information about the supernova of one star: the first row is for the primary (star 1) and the second row for the secondary (star 2), whichever of them explodes first (as for natal_kick_array). Some values in the row of the second supernova take into account the effect of the first (e.g., vsys_1_total is then the total systemic velocity after both supernovae), and its velocities are given in the frame of the first supernova.

What do these kick angles mean?¶

Understanding the reference frame and angles of supernova kicks can be rather confusing (priv. comm. Wagg). Keep reading to gain a better understanding of the geometry of supernova kicks and how they are stored in COSMIC’s kick_info array.

More detailed explanation of euler angles and geometry

The result of a supernova kick depends strongly on the kick magnitude and amount of mass loss at core collapse, but it’s also important to know the orbital phase (\(M\) (mean_anomaly)) and kick direction (\(\theta\) (theta), \(\phi\) (phi)). A kick in the direction of motion at periapsis is more likely to disrupt the binary than a kick of the same magnitude at apoapsis, and a kick out of the orbital plane can tilt the orbit without disrupting it. At the same time, it’s important to account for the fact that the second supernova’s kick is applied in a frame that has already been tilted by the first supernova. The Euler angles describe how the orbit is reoriented by each supernova, and are used to rotate the velocity changes from the second supernova into the frame of the first. theta_euler is the tilt of the orbit, the angle between the orbital angular momentum before (\(\hat{z}\)) and after (\(\hat{h}\)) the supernova. phi_euler is the angle, in the pre-SN orbital plane, from the \(x\)-axis to the line of nodes \(\hat{z} \times \hat{h}\) (where the old and new orbital planes intersect, and the axis about which the orbit tilts). psi_euler (\(\psi_E\)) is the angle, in the post-SN orbital plane, from the line of nodes to the new periapsis of the exploding star’s orbit relative to its companion.

It’s also worth mentioning that beyond the natal kick, the Blaauw kick resulting from symmetric mass loss can also significantly affect the binary’s evolution. The exploding star of mass \(m_1\) loses the ejecta \(\Delta m\) and leaves a remnant of mass \(m_1 - \Delta m\). Without a natal kick, the binary is disrupted if it loses more than \(r/2a\) of its total mass \(M_i = m_1 + m_2\), where \(r\) is the separation at the supernova. For a circular orbit that is half of the total mass; an eccentric orbit is easier to disrupt near periapsis, where the stars move fastest, and harder near apoapsis.

The words above have been known to be confusing to almost all people; the interactive plots below are designed to help out!
